## Conceptual Background: Rating Scales And Cumulative Default Metrics

### S&P Global Ratings’ Long-Term Corporate Rating Scale And Modifiers

To place the requested tables in context, it is useful to recall the structure of S&P Global Ratings’ long-term corporate rating scale. S&P assigns ratings on an ordinal scale ranging from **AAA**, the highest rating signifying extremely strong capacity to meet financial commitments, down through intermediate grades such as **AA**, **A**, and **BBB**, and further into speculative-grade categories such as **BB**, **B**, and **CCC/C**, which denote increasing vulnerability to adverse business, financial, or economic conditions.[12] Within each broad-letter grade, S&P uses plus and minus **modifiers** to differentiate relative standing; for example, AA+, AA, and AA− all lie within the AA category but convey subtly different risk profiles, with AA+ considered at the top end and AA− at the lower end of the AA band.[12] Investment-grade ratings are typically defined as those from **BBB−** and above, encompassing AAA, AA, A, and BBB categories, while speculative-grade ratings cover **BB+** and below, including BB, B, and CCC/C.[12][6]  

The cumulative default-rate tables that you are verifying translate this alphabetic rating scale into empirical measures of default risk by observing, over long historical periods, how often issuers in each rating category default within specified time horizons. Using a **static pool** methodology, S&P forms cohorts of issuers at the beginning of each year, categorized by their current rating, and then tracks the proportion of those issuers that default over horizons of one, two, three, up to fifteen years.[13][11] The cumulative default rate at horizon \(T\) for a given rating notch is typically defined as \(1 - \prod_{t=1}^{T} s_t\), where \(s_t\) denotes the proportion of cohort members surviving without default through year \(t\).[13] In the S&P studies, cumulative default rates are computed on an **issuer-weighted** basis, meaning that each issuer counts equally regardless of its debt volume, and the “weighted average” default rates referred to in the methodology use the number of issuers at the beginning of each year as the basis for the weights.[11]  

A key feature of the modifier-level tables is that they reveal subtle differences in default experience within each broad-letter grade. For example, AAA default rates are typically very close to zero even out to ten years, while AA+ and AA may show slightly higher cumulative default rates due to the somewhat weaker profile of issuers in those categories.[10][12] At the same time, because the AAA cohort tends to be smaller and more select, the sample size available for computing long-horizon default rates can be limited, leading to potential anomalies such as an AA+ five-year cumulative default rate that appears lower than the AAA value in a particular time slice—an artefact of small-sample statistical noise rather than a genuine reversal of risk ordering.[10][12][13] Part of your verification exercise involves recognizing these small-sample effects and interpreting tables accordingly, rather than assuming strictly monotonic relationships between modifiers and default rates.  

### Moody’s Long-Term Rating Scale And Alphanumeric Notches

Moody’s Investors Service uses a similar but distinct rating scale, characterized by **alphanumeric** grades that combine broad-letter categories with numerical modifiers. The highest rating, **Aaa**, is described as obligations judged to be of the highest quality and subject to the lowest level of credit risk, analogous to S&P’s AAA.[14] The next broad-letter grade, **Aa**, indicates obligations of high quality with very low credit risk, subdivided into **Aa1**, **Aa2**, and **Aa3** to capture finer distinctions.[14] Similarly, **A** is upper-medium grade with low credit risk, split into **A1**, **A2**, and **A3**; **Baa** is medium grade with moderate credit risk and may possess some speculative characteristics, with notches **Baa1**, **Baa2**, and **Baa3**.[14] Below investment grade, **Ba** obligations are speculative, **B** obligations are considered speculative and subject to high credit risk, and **Caa** obligations are speculative of poor standing and subject to very high credit risk; these are again divided into numerical modifiers 1, 2, and 3.[14] Finally, **Ca** obligations are highly speculative and likely in or near default, with some prospect of recovery, while **C** obligations are typically in default with little prospect of recovery; for cumulative default statistics, Moody’s often groups Ca and C into a combined **Ca–C** bucket.[14][7]  

Moody’s alphanumeric cumulative default tables, such as Exhibit 26 in the “Corporate Default and Recovery Rates, 1920–2006” special comment and its successors, report the **average cumulative issuer-weighted default rate** for each alphanumeric rating at horizons from one to at least ten years, and often out to fifteen or sixteen years.[17][7] Like S&P, Moody’s defines default events to include not only missed or delayed disbursements of interest or principal (excluding those cured within a contractually allowed grace period) but also bankruptcy filings or legal receiverships that are expected to result in missed contractual payments, and **distressed exchanges** whereby an issuer offers creditors restructured debt or new securities that entail a diminished financial obligation relative to the original contract.[14] By aggregating defaults across cohorts over several decades, Moody’s constructs a long-run empirical mapping from alphanumeric ratings to observed default frequencies, which then serves as an anchor for risk estimation, pricing, and capital allocation in corporate credit markets.[8][17]  

### Cumulative Default Rates Versus Point-In-Time Probabilities

Both S&P’s and Moody’s cumulative default-rate tables represent historical **frequencies of default** over specified horizons, rather than **forward-looking one-year default probabilities** at a given point in time. This distinction is important for interpreting and verifying tables. A cumulative five-year default rate of, say, 0.50% for a given rating notch means that, on average over the historical sample, one in 200 issuers starting in that rating notch default within five years, assuming no rating migration or withdrawal effects in the counting rule.[10][17] It does not necessarily imply that the **current one-year forward-looking probability of default** is 0.10% per year, as such inferences would require additional assumptions about stationarity, hazard rates, and the shape of the default intensity term structure.  

Moody’s explicitly distinguishes between **default rates** and **probabilities of default (PDs)**, noting that its PD measures used in credit risk models and structured finance ratings often look one year ahead and are derived from a combination of historical data, macroeconomic outlook, and issuer-specific information.[8] For example, a Moody’s report discussing a “growth recession” hounding parts of the corporate credit market emphasizes that Moody’s PDs for speculative-grade companies as of the end of 2024 look one year ahead into 2025, and that credit conditions in 2025 will depend on the evolution of macroeconomic drivers and corporate balance sheets.[8] Historical cumulative default rates, by contrast, provide a **benchmark** against which current PDs can be compared, but they are not identical to those PDs, nor do they incorporate the same forward-looking information.  

The **idealized** cumulative expected loss and default probability tables used by Moody’s in structured finance methodologies occupy a hybrid position between historical frequencies and forward-looking PDs. They are constructed to reflect Moody’s view of the appropriate **risk level** consistent with each rating under long-run conditions, but they are not direct historical averages; instead, they are calibrated to align with thresholds that Moody’s considers acceptable for exposures bearing a given rating.[9] This is why, in the BIS analysis, Moody’s idealized default probabilities and expected losses are used as **target benchmarks** to determine whether modelled tranche losses are consistent with the desired rating.[9]

## Methodological Considerations And Caveats

### Static-Pool Construction, Survivorship, And Cumulative Rates

Both S&P and Moody’s compute cumulative default rates using a **static-pool** approach, in which issuers are grouped into cohorts based on their rating at the beginning of an observation period and then tracked over time.[13][17] S&P’s methodology, as described in the 2012 study, involves dividing the number of issuers in a static pool that default by a specific time horizon by the number of issuers that survived (did not default) to that point in time.[13] The cumulative default rate is calculated as **one minus the product of the proportion of survivors** across all years up to the horizon of interest.[13] This approach ensures that the cumulative rate reflects the compounding effect of default risk over time, taking into account that some issuers may default earlier and thus cannot default again at later horizons.  

Moody’s similarly bases its cumulative default and recovery statistics on issuer-weighted averages, focusing on senior unsecured bond recovery rates for defaulted issuers and tracking default events across rating cohorts.[17] In Moody’s methodology, each issuer contributes equally to the aggregate default rate for its rating category, regardless of the amount of debt outstanding, which avoids concentration effects but may underweight the experience of large issuers relative to their market impact.[17] By using issuer-weighted measures, both agencies emphasize **frequency** of default over **volume** of defaulted debt, which is appropriate for calibrating rating scales but may differ from risk measures used in portfolio analytics that weight by exposure.  

### Rating Transitions And Withdrawals

An important caveat when interpreting cumulative default tables is that issuers may **migrate** between ratings over time or have their ratings withdrawn due to events such as corporate restructuring, mergers, or market exit. The static-pool methodology typically classifies issuers by their rating at the **start** of the observation period and then tracks defaults regardless of subsequent rating changes, so an issuer rated BBB at the beginning of the period that later migrates to BB and then defaults is counted as a BBB cohort default.[10][13][17] This ensures that the cumulative default rate reflects the risk level associated with the starting rating, inclusive of transitions.  

However, rating withdrawals complicate the analysis, as issuers whose ratings are withdrawn for reasons other than default must be handled carefully to avoid bias. Some methodologies treat withdrawals as survivors for purposes of cumulative default-rate calculation, while others adjust for censoring.[13][17] The precise treatment can affect long-horizon cumulative default rates, especially in higher-rated cohorts where defaults are rare and withdrawals may constitute a significant fraction of the sample. In your verification work, if you are matching the S&P and Moody’s tables, it is prudent to confirm the agencies’ current treatment of withdrawals and transitions in their methodology sections, even if you do not directly compute the rates yourself.  

### Small-Sample Effects And Non-Monotonicity Across Modifiers

One of the caveats you explicitly mention is the possibility that a rating modifier like **AA+** might exhibit a lower cumulative default rate than **AAA** over a given horizon, contrary to the intuitive expectation that AAA should be the lowest-risk notch. Such anomalies can occur due to **small-sample effects** and the specific composition of cohorts over the historical period. When the number of AAA-rated corporate issuers is small, especially in earlier years of the sample, the cumulative default rate at long horizons can be sensitive to a handful of default events.[10][12][13]  

For example, suppose that only a few dozen issuers were rated AAA in the early part of the period and that one or two of them defaulted over a 10-year horizon due to idiosyncratic shocks. The resulting cumulative default rate might be higher than that observed in the larger AA+ cohort, which could contain many more issuers and spread default events more thinly across the sample. This does not imply that AA+ is fundamentally safer than AAA; rather, it reflects the statistical noise inherent in estimating small probabilities from finite samples. S&P and Moody’s typically draw attention to such issues in their methodological notes, and structured finance practitioners often treat anomalies in the empirical tables with caution, using idealized scales as smoother benchmarks.[9][10][12]

### Idealized Versus Empirical Default Rates And Expected Losses

Finally, the distinction between **idealized** and **empirical** scales is crucial for interpreting Moody’s idealized cumulative expected loss and default probability tables. Empirical cumulative default rates are based on historical issuer-weighted frequencies; idealized scales, by contrast, represent Moody’s **target risk levels** for ratings under normal conditions, and may be smoother and more monotonic than the empirical data.[9][17] This is particularly important in structured finance contexts, where the modelled expected loss for a tranche needs to be compared to an idealized threshold corresponding to the desired rating.  

For example, if the empirical five-year cumulative default rate for Aaa-rated corporate issuers over 1983–2023 is 0.20%, Moody’s idealized Aaa five-year expected loss threshold might be lower or higher depending on how Moody’s calibrates its long-run risk tolerance for Aaa ratings.[9] The idealized scale may also reflect considerations such as **rating stability**, **migration patterns**, and **systemic stress scenarios**, which are not captured in raw historical frequencies.[8][9][14] As a result, when verifying a research note that references both empirical cumulative default tables and idealized expected loss matrices, you should carefully distinguish which numbers come from historical studies and which come from rating methodology documents, and avoid mixing them in ways that could misrepresent the agencies’ intended risk mapping.
